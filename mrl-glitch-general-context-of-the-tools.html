General Context

1. Conceptual map: VR, AR, MR, XR, “extended reality”

Virtual Reality (VR)

  • Fully digital environment.
  • The real world is blocked out; you see only a virtual scene.
  • Tracking usually includes head + hands/controllers; sometimes full body.
  • Typical use: games, simulations, training, narrative worlds.

Augmented Reality (AR)

  • You see the real world first. Digital elements are overlaid on top.
  • Classic AR is “additive”: it doesn’t really understand or occlude the world, just draws on top (e.g., early phone AR, simple head-up displays).

Mixed Reality (MR)

  • Also shows the real world, but with spatial understanding:
    • planes, depth, surfaces, sometimes hands and body;
    • virtual objects can collide with tables, hide behind real objects, stick to walls, etc.
  • Headsets like Meta Quest 3 and Apple Vision Pro use color passthrough cameras + depth/mesh APIs to do this.

XR / “Extended Reality”

  • Umbrella term that covers VR + AR + MR and all hybrids.
  • “Extended reality” is basically marketing-speak for “any reality with headsets / glasses in the mix”.
  • So when someone says “extended reality”, they’re not contradicting “mixed reality”; they’re placing MR (and VR, AR) inside a larger conceptual bucket.
Summary:

XR = umbrella
VR = fully virtual
AR = real world + simple overlay
MR = real world + spatially aware, interactive virtual layer

2. Meta Quest 3 in 2025: position and highlights

Timeline & basics

  • Lineage: Oculus → Meta Quest. Meta acquired Oculus in 2014; Quest 1 (2019), Quest 2 (2020), Quest 3 (2023).
  • Quest 3 release: 10 October 2023.
  • OS: Meta Horizon OS, a custom Android (currently based on Android 14).

Hardware snapshot

  • Standalone headset (no PC or console required), but can also connect to PC via Link / Air Link to run SteamVR etc.
  • SoC: Qualcomm Snapdragon XR2 Gen 2, roughly ~2× GPU performance vs Quest 2, with 8 GB RAM.
  • Display: LCD, 2064×2208 px per eye, up to 120 Hz, pancake lenses → slimmer form factor and better clarity.
  • Color passthrough cameras + depth sensor → supports room meshing, depth API, believable MR occlusion.

Price & cost–benefit (2023–2025)

  • Launch price: $499 (128 GB) and $649 (512 GB), marketed as “the world’s first mass-market mixed reality headset.”
  • By 2024–2025:
    • Meta introduced a cheaper Quest 3S (same chip, simpler optics) around $299.
    • Meta is working on a future Quest 4 as a higher-end successor, expected around 2026.
  • Even with price fluctuations, Quest 3 sits as the mid-range “reference” headset:
    • much cheaper than Apple Vision Pro,
    • more capable than older Quest 2 and most low-end mobile VR,
    • fully standalone (unlike PS VR2, which requires a PS5).

Position vs competitors (2025)

  • Compared to Apple Vision Pro
    • Vision Pro: ultra-high-end, eye tracking, micro-OLED displays, Mac integration, much higher price.
    • Quest 3: lower resolution but still sharp, mass-market pricing, huge games catalog, more approachable for experimentation and casual use.
  • Compared to PS VR2 / PC-tethered headsets
    • PS VR2: excellent visuals and haptics but needs PS5 and is pure VR (no full-color passthrough MR in the same way).
    • PC VR (HTC Vive, Index): often higher fidelity via powerful GPUs, but more complex setup and no standalone mode.
    • Quest 3: sweet spot of mobility + MR + cost; it can also act as a wireless PC VR headset via Wi-Fi 6E.

As a creative / dev tool

  • Strong support for Unity and Unreal with official Meta XR SDKs, samples, and “building blocks” for hands, passthrough, spatial anchors, mesh APIs, etc.
  • Runs completely untethered, so you can build:
    • site-specific MR pieces in galleries, classrooms, outdoors (if safe);
    • prototypes that don’t depend on a PC nearby.
  • Very healthy store ecosystem: billions in Quest Store revenue overall → a real publishing channel for experimental games, apps, or tools.
Summary

Quest 3 (2023–2025) = mid-range, mass-market MR headset — Snapdragon XR2 Gen 2, standalone, color passthrough, strong Unity/Unreal support, good enough optics, huge ecosystem.

3. Unity – history + strengths (for XR / creative tech)

History (very condensed)

  • Originated as a project by three Danish devs (David Helgason, Joachim Ante, Nicholas Francis) around 2002–2004 under the company Over the Edge Entertainment (OTEE) in Denmark.
  • Unity 1.0 released June 2005 at Apple’s WWDC as a Mac-only game engine.
  • Then expanded to Windows, web, consoles, and later mobile; became huge with iPhone (2007–2008) by making mobile 3D dev accessible.
  • Today (2025): latest LTS line is Unity 6000.x LTS, still focused on cross-platform games, apps, and XR.

What Unity is good at (esp. for creative tech & MR)

  • Rapid prototyping & experimentation
    • Short iteration loops, tons of tutorials, assets, templates.
    • Great fit for “creative technologist wants to try a weird idea in a weekend”.
  • Mobile + standalone XR
    • Very mature Android / Quest toolchain; most Quest titles are built in Unity.
  • Component-based design
    • Easy to wire behaviours via components, C#, visual scripting, and to reuse “prefabs”.
  • Ecosystem
    • Asset Store, 3rd-party tools (generative art tools, VFX, shaders, etc.), integrations with sensors, MIDI, OSC, etc.

For Meta Quest 3 specifically:

  • Unity + Meta XR SDK gives you:
    • prefabs for camera rigs, hands, interactions;
    • APIs for passthrough, depth/meshing, spatial anchors;
    • MR “building blocks” that let you stand up interactive prototypes fast.

4. Unreal Engine – history + strengths (for XR / creative tech)

History (very condensed)

  • Developed by Epic Games founder Tim Sweeney starting around 1995 for the game Unreal.
  • Unreal Engine 1 powers Unreal (1998) and later Unreal Tournament, then gets licensed to other studios.
  • UE2/UE3 dominate high-end PC/console shooters and action games in the 2000s.
  • Unreal Engine 4 (2014) brings modern PBR graphics, Blueprints visual scripting, and a friendlier licensing model.
  • Unreal Engine 5 (2021→) adds Nanite (virtualized geometry) and Lumen (global illumination), pushing near-cinematic real-time graphics; by 2025, UE5 is the reference for high-fidelity real-time 3D across games, film, and virtual production.

What Unreal is good at (esp. for XR / MR)

  • Visual fidelity / cinematic graphics
    • Ideal when you want realistic lighting, massive scenes, film-like look.
  • Virtual production & architecture
    • Widely used in studios for LED volumes, previs, archviz, automotive demos.
  • Blueprints
    • Node-based scripting that lets artists/technical artists do complex logic without writing C++.

For XR / Quest-style devices:

  • Unreal has XR templates and plugins for Meta Quest, but historically has been more dominant in PC-tethered or high-end standalone content (Quest can run UE, but many devs prefer Unity there for speed & tooling).
  • Strong choice if your creative goal is cinematic MR or very polished visuals, or if you’re already in the Unreal ecosystem (film, archviz, game studios).

5. Unity vs Unreal for Meta Quest 3 / MR, conceptually

This is not about “better engine”, but different personalities:

Unity + Quest 3

  • Feels like: fast sketchbook / lab for XR.
  • Best for:
    • rapid MR experiments, interactive installations, generative pieces;
    • educational apps, playful prototypes, standing/room-scale toys.
  • Pros:
    • lighter editor, very mature Quest pipeline, huge community of Quest tutorials;
    • Asset Store and Meta’s Building Blocks help you get MR + hands working quickly.

Unreal + Quest 3

  • Feels like: film set / high-end studio.
  • Best for:
    • visually ambitious MR experiences;
    • projects that may also target PC/console, virtual production, or cinematic workflows.
  • Pros:
    • Unreal’s rendering + tools (Sequencer, Nanite, Lumen) are very powerful when visual polish is a priority.

For a “creative technology” audience

  • Unity:
    • Lower friction to learn; great for workshops, quick experiments, interactive art.
    • Strong combination with Quest 3 when the goal is exploring ideas (e.g., body-based interfaces, data-driven visuals in MR).
  • Unreal:
    • Stronger when the project demands cinema-level visuals or must integrate with film / broadcast pipelines.
    • Good for teams where artists already live in Unreal (VFX/film).

You can absolutely mix them in your practice:

  • Prototype concepts, interaction, and MR behaviours in Unity + Quest 3.
  • For selected pieces that need a “wow visual factor” or are tied to film/TV, port or rebuild in Unreal.

What is the FBX format?

FBX (Filmbox) is a file format developed by Autodesk. It is a multi-purpose container widely used to store 3D models, animations, textures, and even entire scenes. FBX is well supported in both Unreal Engine and Unity, and is often used as an “exchange format” between the two, even though there can be subtle differences depending on how the file was exported.

What an FBX file can contain:

  • Geometry (3D models)
  • Animations (object or character motion)
  • Textures and materials
  • Lights and cameras
  • Rigging and skeletons (for characters)
  • Animation curves / paths (for characters, cameras, or objects)

What to do with FBX in Unity?

Unity can read FBX files natively, and they can be imported directly into your project. General workflow:

  1. Drag the FBX file into the Assets folder of your Unity project.
  2. Unity will automatically import the 3D model, animations, and textures (if they are embedded or correctly referenced).
  3. In the Inspector, you can adjust the FBX import settings (scale, rig, animation import, materials, etc.).
  4. If the FBX contains animations, you can set up animation controllers in Unity to drive those clips.
  5. Once imported, the FBX can be placed into the scene, and you can attach effects, scripts, or interactions on top of it.
